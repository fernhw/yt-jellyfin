Scrum for indies — the analogue way

What is GYRA?

GYRA is a Scrum board for indies. Free. Made by people who actually use it.

It is the anti-Jira. It is also the anti-bad-software-design. Software pretends a board is rows in a database. A board is not rows. A board is the wall in the room where the team stands every morning and looks at the same thing together.

Before the cloud took everything, a board was paper. Standup meant getting up and walking over. You looked at the cards on the wall, you saw the whole picture at once, and the act of standing there was the meeting. The ritual was the work.

GYRA brings the wall back. The colors are warm. The cards look like post-its. They tilt. They get stickers stuck on them. They live on the board long enough to feel earned. Open it in fullscreen on a 100-inch screen with a gyro mouse and it becomes the wall again — shared, physical, in the room.

That is the point. Tools shape behavior. A spreadsheet makes you behave like a spreadsheet. GYRA makes you behave like a team standing in front of a board.

GYRA is free for indies. Built by High Ground. Take it, use it, share it.

What is Scrum?

Scrum is small batches.

Pick a fixed window — one week, two weeks. Inside the window, you ship something real. Then you stop, look at what you built, and decide what is next. Repeat.

That is the entire framework. Everything else is service to it.

The three things

  1. Sprints. A fixed deadline. Short. Non-negotiable.
  2. User Stories. One shippable slice of value. Not a task.
  3. Points. A relative size, not a clock estimate.

Plans that look five years out are fiction. Plans that look two weeks out get built. That is why Scrum works and waterfall does not.

User Stories — GYRA's Format

A user story is one sentence. It says who gets something, where they do it, and what changes for them when it works.

That is the whole point: a person who has never seen the project can read a single card and understand who it is for and why it exists. If a card needs a meeting to explain it, it is not a story yet.

Forget “As a / I want / so that” — nobody talks that way. GYRA gives you six slots. Fill them like a sentence.

[actor]  [needs]  [this]  [where]  [to / for]  [what]

The six slots

SlotWhat goes here
actorWho receives the value. User or Admin. That is it.
needsThe desire word. needs, wants, must, can, should be able to…
thisA single observable action — a verb. crawl, press button, save, jump. What the user actually does.
whereWhere in the product it happens. Main Menu, Combat Sandbox, Settings page.
to / forThe connector. to for outcomes, for for purposes. because, so they, so we, in order to also work for longer phrasing.
whatThe outcome — what is different for the user once this works.

The user's perspective — the most important rule

A story is always written from the side of the person who receives the value. Never the person who builds it.

This sounds obvious until you write one. Then everyone does this:

  • “Designer needs to tune enemy damage” — wrong. The designer is on the team. The story has no user.
  • “Developer needs a save endpoint” — wrong. The endpoint is plumbing. Who is it actually for?

Rewrite from the receiver:

  • User needs fight tuned enemies, Combat Sandbox, for balanced game combat. — right. You can tell if it shipped.
  • User needs save progress, Main Menu, to choose what to do later. — right. A person feels the result.

If the only person affected by a story is someone on the team, it is a task, not a story. Put it in the description, not the title. The one exception: a tool genuinely built for another staff member — then that staffer is the user. Otherwise the actor is User or Admin.

Real examples

  • User needs save progress, Main Menu Demo Version, to choose what to do later.
  • User needs fight tuned enemies, Combat Sandbox, for balanced game combat.
  • Admin needs ban accounts, Moderator Console, to clean up after attacks.
  • User wants reset password, Login Screen, to recover without writing to support.

How GYRA helps you write better — the gentle nudges

GYRA will not block a sloppy story. It saves it and warns you. The warnings come from decades of teams making the same mistakes:

  • Solutioning. “this” starts with add, create, build, implement, design, refactor, wire, integrate, deploy… — that is build work, not a user action. The user does not “add a button.” The user presses a button.
  • Two stories in one. “this” or “what” contains and, &, or a comma. Split it. Small stories ship; bundled ones rot.
  • Vague outcome. “what” says make it work, nice ux, better, stuff, reasons. That is filler. Say what actually changes for the user.
  • Circular. The outcome just restates the action: save progress / to save their progress. The outcome should explain why, not repeat what.
  • Too big. Over 8 points triggers a warning. See the Story Points section — if you cannot size it, split it.
  • Untestable. A story sized 3+ with no description has no way to know it is done. Add at least one “done when…” line.
  • Noun where a verb belongs. “this” starting with a, the, tuning, tuned… is usually a noun phrase pretending to be an action. Lead with the verb.

Hard rules (these block the save)

  • Actor must be User or Admin. Anything else is rejected.
  • All six slots required.
  • Full sentence: 19 words max across all six slots together.
  • If you cannot say it in this shape, the idea is not a story yet — it is research, a task, or a wish. Put it somewhere else.
The test: read the card to someone who has never seen the project. If they understand who it is for and what changes for that person when it ships, the story works. If you have to explain it, rewrite it.

Story Points

Points are not hours. Points are sizes, compared to each other.

If you have never seen this before, here is the shift:

People are bad at guessing how long work will take. Decades of evidence. “This will take four hours” is almost always wrong. It ignores interruptions, edge cases, the bug you find halfway in, and the optimism you applied before touching the code.

People are good at comparing two things side by side. Which apple is bigger? Easy. Which of these two stories is the bigger lift? Also easy — even when you have no clue how many hours either will take.

That is the whole trick: stop guessing time, start comparing sizes. The team picks one story everyone agrees on as a baseline, calls it a 3. Every other story is sized by asking “is this bigger or smaller than that one, and by how much?”

After a few sprints the team learns roughly how many points fit in a sprint. That number is honest, because it came from real shipping — not from optimism. You plan with it.

The scale

Use modified Fibonacci. The gaps grow on purpose, so you cannot pretend a 5 and a 6 are different things. You either round down or admit it is the next size up.

PointsWhat it means
1Trivial. Change a color. Fix a typo.
2Small. A button. A query. A component.
3Medium. A form with validation. An endpoint. A modal.
5Large. A feature with multiple states and edge cases.
8Very large. A subsystem. An integration. A new screen. The biggest a story should be when it enters a sprint.
13Too big — GYRA warns you. Split it before it enters the sprint.
21You do not understand it well enough yet. Stop. Break it down.

A 5 is not five hours. A 5 is bigger than a 3 and smaller than an 8. That is all you need.

Why comparing works when guessing does not

  • It takes the time pressure out of the estimate. Nobody is being asked “will you finish today?”
  • It surfaces disagreement. If one person says 2 and another says 8, you have just found a hidden assumption. Talk about it (see Planning Poker).
  • It gets more accurate over time. After three sprints your point-per-sprint number is real, not a hope.
  • It catches stories that are too big. An “I do not know” is itself information — that story needs to be split or researched first.
Velocity is total points your team finishes in a sprint. It is a planning number. Not a grade. Never use it to compare people or pressure the team.

Refinement — Why You Should Not Fill Points or Priority Alone

When you import stories from a spreadsheet, GYRA tells you to leave the points and priority columns blank. That is on purpose.

Both numbers are conversations, not facts. They only mean something when the team agrees on them together. A points value you typed alone in a spreadsheet at 1am is just your guess wearing a costume.

What refinement is, in plain words

The team sits down for fifteen or thirty minutes, walks through new stories one at a time, and for each one decides three things together:

  1. Is the story clear? Does everyone understand who it is for and what changes when it ships? If not, rewrite or split.
  2. How big is it? Size it by comparison (see Story Points). Everyone votes at once; disagreement is information.
  3. How important is it? The Product Owner sets priority while the team explains the cost. Both halves of that picture are needed.

A story is “ready” once it is clear, sized, prioritised, and has at least one “done when…” line. Stories that are not ready do not enter the sprint. That single rule kills 80% of sprint pain.

GYRA's Grooming view — built for this exact moment

Open Grooming in the sidebar. It is a real-time, multi-player room: every teammate sees the same story, casts a points vote at the same time, and watches the others land. No one anchors the vote. Disagreements surface immediately. Talk for thirty seconds, re-vote, move on.

Everything updates live across every screen. The Product Owner can shuffle priority while the team is voting on size, and both numbers settle together. When the story is ready, one click pushes it into the sprint backlog.

This is the one part of the tool we are most proud of. Most teams have never had it. They use a spreadsheet, or a Zoom call where everyone takes turns saying numbers, or a plugin bolted onto another tracker that is worse than both. Grooming in GYRA is in the box and built so a five-person studio can refine a sprint's worth of stories in a single coffee.

Why we built this: Jira is an advanced tool aimed at teams that already know Scrum cold. Most teams do not — they pick up Jira and inherit forty fields, twelve states, and zero context for any of it. GYRA is the opposite. The tool teaches the practice. Grooming is the most concentrated dose of that idea: a single screen that makes the right behaviour the path of least resistance.

What “ready” actually looks like

  • Six story slots filled and the sentence makes sense out loud.
  • Points set by team vote — not one person, not the loudest person, not last sprint's leftover.
  • Priority set by the Product Owner, in the open, while the team watches.
  • At least one acceptance line (“done when the user can press X and see Y”).
  • No “and” smashing two stories together. If there is, split first, refine after.

That is it. Refinement is not a meeting people dread. Done right, it is the cheapest insurance the team buys all sprint.

Planning Poker

Everyone reveals their estimate at the same time. No one talks first. That is the whole rule.

The reason: if the most senior person says “3” first, everyone says 3. You learn nothing. When the numbers come out together and one person said 1 and another said 8, you have just found an assumption gap. Talk about that. Re-estimate.

The disagreement is the value, not the number.

In GYRA you do this in Grooming, in the sidebar. A story does not enter a sprint until it is groomed: pointed, with acceptance criteria, with a priority. Ungroomed stories rot.

Why Iterate

Perfect is the enemy of shipped.

Build a small thing. Put it in front of real eyes, even just your own. Learn what was wrong. Build the next small thing. Repeat. The compound effect over six months is the difference between a product and a graveyard of plans.

One sprint where you ship something beats six months of design documents. Every time. The reasons stack:

  • You find out the idea was wrong before you built three systems on top of it.
  • Morale stays up when there is always something tangible to show.
  • Sprint two can change direction based on sprint one. A waterfall plan cannot.
  • “Great idea — into the backlog, revisit next sprint” defends scope better than any meeting.
  • Real feedback teaches you what design docs cannot.

The backlog is not a failure bin

The backlog is everything the product could become. It grows constantly. Most items will never be built. That is correct. It means you were building the right ones instead.

Multiple Assignees — The Slice of Cake

A real feature is a slice of cake. Top to bottom. Every layer.

“Make Character” is not a story. It is a project. A real story is User needs control a living character, in the Open World, to feel like they are inside the game. That story needs concept design, modeling, texturing, rigging, animation, code to load the model, code to drive it, sound for the footsteps, and someone to test that it all works together. No single person ships that. A Jira ticket assigned to one person ships none of it.

GYRA assigns the team to the slice. The slice is the unit. Everyone on the slice iterates together: the rigger sees the animator's tests, the animator sees the code wiring, the coder sees the design intent. The story does not move forward in a relay race — it moves forward as a body.

This is the mini-waterfall: each story is its own little top-to-bottom delivery. Inside the sprint, you have many of these slices running in parallel, each with its own small team. Iteration becomes the default. Working together becomes the default. The team is the unit of work, not the individual.

One person per ticket = a feature that no one finishes. A slice of cake needs every layer. Assign every layer.

The Rugby Scrum — Why Stories Belong to the Team

The word “Scrum” comes from rugby. In a rugby scrum the whole pack binds together and moves as one mass. No player decides where the pack goes. The pack goes, together, or not at all. Sutherland used that word on purpose.

Jira, Linear, and the rest got this wrong. They forced one owner per ticket. They turned a collective framework into a grid of individual to-do lists that happen to share a deadline. The pack falls apart the second one person “owns” the card and everyone else waits.

GYRA assigns the team to the story because that is what Scrum actually is. When anyone can help on any story, stories move. When someone is blocked, anyone steps in. When the sprint ends, the team finished it. That is the only definition of done that matters.

Scrum has roles. They matter.

  • Scrum Master. Protects the team's focus. Removes blockers the team cannot remove alone. Runs the ceremonies. Not a manager. Not a ticket-assigner. A shield.
  • Product Owner. Owns the priority of the backlog. Decides what done means. Speaks for the stakeholders so the team is not pulled in ten directions. Decides the what; the team decides the how.
  • Development Team. Self-organizes. Pulls work from the top of the backlog. Decides together how to build it. No senior dev handing tickets down.

In a two-person studio all three roles may be you, on different days. That is fine. The roles are functions, not job titles. GYRA strips the enterprise overhead — approval gates, ticket-routing, permission layers — and keeps the functions.

What GYRA fixes is the tool assumption that one story belongs to one person. That assumption breaks the scrum. GYRA gives it back.

The Board, Stickers, Post-its — Why GYRA Looks Analogue

Users are human. Every line of GYRA is built around that fact.

A card lives on the board for weeks. You stand in front of it every morning. You look at it. Eventually you stop seeing it. That is a failure of the tool, not the team. GYRA fights that.

Cards look like post-its because they are post-its

Slight rotation. Warm paper colors. A small shadow underneath. The actor and the action read first. They stack. They tilt. They look used.

That is not nostalgia. That is utility. A post-it gives the card a body. You remember where it sits, what it looks like, how long it has been there. A flat row in a database gives you none of that.

Stickers

Pull the sticker menu from the top of the board: Alert, Arrow, Question, Star, Fire, Review, Done, Blocked, Escalate, Note. Slap them on a card. Pin them anywhere on the wall. Add a label. Drag them around.

They stay. When the card archives, the stickers archive with it. A card covered in stickers carries its own history — the fire it caught, the question that took three days, the blocker that finally cleared. That mess has value. It says: this card mattered, things happened on it.

Cover your cards. Cover your wall. The mess is the memory.

Fullscreen

Click the fullscreen button. The chrome disappears. The board fills the screen. It is meant for a 100-inch display in the room, a gyro mouse in someone's hand, the team standing in front of it. Standup becomes a ritual again. Not a Zoom grid of faces. A wall.

Screensaver

The Screensaver button cycles team stories full-screen, one at a time. Big. Slow. Quiet. Leave it running on the studio TV. Anyone walking past sees what the team is on. The work becomes ambient.

Software is not the work. The work is the work. GYRA tries to disappear into the wall, the cards, the post-its, the team. The tool is a board. Treat it like one.

Reading the board honestly

  • Everything stuck in In Progress — something is blocked or the scope is too big.
  • Nothing in Done by mid-sprint — stories are too large. Split them.
  • One person carrying eight cards — redistribute. The sprint will fail.
  • The board does not match reality — update the board. It only works when it is true.

The Retrospective

At the end of every sprint, run a retro. Three questions: What went well? What did not? What changes next sprint? Five honest minutes beats any process document.

The Retro view is in the sidebar. Use it every sprint. Even solo. Especially solo.
