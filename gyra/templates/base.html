What is GYRA?

GYRA is a free Scrum board built for indie developers, small studios, and solo creators. Think of it as Jira's dark mirror — the same structural power, none of the corporate bloat, none of the pricing tiers.

If you've never used a project management tool before: welcome. You're about to see why Scrum changed how software gets made. GYRA was designed specifically to make that framework accessible to people working outside of enterprise teams — meaning you can run a real sprint without needing a Product Owner, a Scrum Master certificate, or a $50/seat/month subscription.

GYRA is free for Indies. Made by High Ground because good tools shouldn't require an enterprise budget.

What is Scrum?

Scrum is a lightweight framework for delivering work in short, focused bursts called sprints. Instead of planning a year-long project all at once (and watching that plan collapse by week two), Scrum says: plan two weeks, build two weeks, ship something, learn, repeat.

No grand plan survives contact with reality. Scrum makes peace with that.

The three core ideas

  1. Work in sprints (usually 1–2 weeks). A sprint has a fixed end date — you work toward it, you ship something, and you reflect.
  2. Every piece of work is a User Story — a small, shippable thing that delivers real value to someone.
  3. Stories get points — a relative size estimate, not a time estimate. More on this shortly.

That's the whole framework. Everything else in Scrum — ceremonies, roles, velocity — is in service of these three ideas.

User Stories — GYRA's Format

GYRA doesn't use the traditional “As a / I want / so that” format. It enforces a tighter, grammar-structured sentence built from six specific parts, assembled with dropdowns and constrained text fields.

[Actor]  [needs/wants/must]  [Action Word]  [what/feature]  [to/because/so they]  [outcome]

The six parts

FieldTypeWhat goes here
ActorDropdownUser, Developer, Admin, Guest, Manager — who this story serves
VerbFree textThe relationship: needs, wants, must, should have
Action WordSingle word (enforced)A gerund — the thing being built: Walking, Uploading, Deleting, Sorting. GYRA rejects spaces here — one word only.
FeatureFree textWhat the action applies to: animation frames, user accounts, payment receipts
ConnectorDropdownto, because, so they
OutcomeFree textWhy it matters: the game feels alive, get a refund without calling support

Real examples

  • User needs Walking Animation to make the world feel alive
  • Developer wants Automated Testing to catch regressions before deploy
  • Admin must Bulk Delete accounts to remove bots after an attack
  • Guest wants Password Reset to recover their account without support

The Action Word is displayed bold on the board card — it is the core of the story at a glance. It must be a single gerund. If you find yourself typing two words, your story probably needs to be two stories.

Hard rules GYRA enforces

  • The Action Word must be one word with no spaces — the server rejects it otherwise
  • The full sentence must be 19 words or fewer
  • All six parts must be filled in
  • If the story won't fit in a sprint, split it into smaller stories before adding it

Story Points

Story points are not hours. This is the most important thing to internalize about Scrum estimation.

Estimating “this will take 4 hours” is almost always wrong — it ignores unknowns, interruptions, context-switching, and the optimism bias your brain applies to every estimate before you've actually touched the code. Estimating “this is about the same size as the login screen we built last sprint” is honest and surprisingly accurate over time.

Points use a modified Fibonacci scale. The gaps grow intentionally to prevent false precision:

PointsWhat it means in practice
1Trivial — change a color, fix a typo, update copy, tweak a config
2Small — a new button, a simple DB query, a CSS component
3Medium — a form with validation, a new API endpoint, a modal
5Large — a feature with multiple states, branching logic, edge cases
8Very large — a whole subsystem, a third-party integration, a new screen
13Huge — this probably needs to be split before it enters a sprint
21Epic — definitely split this. You don't know enough yet to build it whole.

A 5-point story isn't “5 hours” — it's “meaningfully bigger than a 3-pointer, but not yet in large-system territory.” The fuzziness is intentional. It prevents debates about whether something is 3 hours or 3.5.

Velocity — the total points your team completes per sprint — is your planning compass, not a performance grade. Never use velocity to judge output or compare people. Use it to answer one question: “realistically, how many points can we commit to next sprint?”

Planning Poker

When estimating in a team, everyone should reveal their point estimate simultaneously — so no one anchors off someone else's guess. Each person names a number at the same time. Then you discuss the outliers. Re-estimate if the discussion changes minds.

In GYRA, you do this during Grooming (accessible from the sidebar). Groom your backlog before stories enter a sprint: add acceptance criteria, point them, and order them by priority. Only well-groomed stories should enter a sprint.

The secret of Planning Poker: the disagreements are the entire point. When one person says “1” and another says “8,” you've just discovered an assumption gap. That conversation — before any code is written — is worth more than the estimate itself.

Why Iterate? (The Most Important Section)

Perfect is the enemy of shipped.

Iteration means: build a small thing, put it in front of real people (or at least yourself), learn what was wrong, and make it better next sprint. Repeat indefinitely. The compound effect of this over months is dramatic.

One two-week sprint where you ship something beats six months of planning and never shipping. Every single time. The benefits are real and compounding:

  • Early feedback — find out your idea was wrong before you've built three other systems on top of it
  • Visible progress — morale stays high when you're always shipping something tangible
  • Adaptability — sprint two can pivot based on what you learned in sprint one. You can't do that with a waterfall plan.
  • Scope creep defense — “that's a great idea, let's put it in the backlog and revisit next sprint” is the most powerful phrase in product development
  • Faster learning — every shipped thing teaches you something a design doc never could

The Backlog is not a failure bin

The backlog is your prioritized list of everything the product could become. Things get added constantly. Many will never get built — and that's perfectly fine, because it means you were building the right things instead. A healthy backlog grows faster than it shrinks. That's not a problem; that's a product.

Multiple Assignees Per Story

GYRA lets you assign multiple people to a single story. This is intentional, and it matters more than it sounds.

In enterprise tools, a ticket has one owner. In indie dev, that's often a polite fiction — two people pair on a feature, one implements while another reviews and deploys, or the solo dev is simultaneously the designer, backend engineer, and QA. GYRA reflects how small teams actually work rather than how org charts say they should work.

Multiple assignees also means shared accountability. Nobody owns a story in isolation — the whole team owns the sprint together. If a story stalls, everyone can see who it's with and anyone can pick it up. There's no waiting for the “ticket owner” to respond.

When a story has one assigned person and they go quiet, the story goes quiet too. Multiple assignees create a natural safety net — and signal that help is expected and welcome.

The Rugby Scrum — Why GYRA Assigns Stories to the Team

The word “Scrum” comes from rugby. In a rugby scrum, the whole pack binds together and moves as a single mass. No individual player decides where the unit pushes — the whole team commits together and wins together. That is the metaphor Jeff Sutherland had in mind when he named the framework.

Jira, Linear, and most project management tools got this exactly wrong. By forcing a single owner on every ticket, they turned a collective framework into a grid of individual to-do lists that happen to share a deadline. The scrum breaks the moment one person “owns” a card and everyone else waits on them. That is a task tracker with Scrum branding — not Scrum.

GYRA assigns multiple people to stories intentionally, because that is the actual point. When anyone on the team can help on any story, stories move faster. When someone is blocked, anyone can step in without a reassignment ceremony. When the sprint closes, the team finished — not a list of individuals.

Scrum does have roles. They matter. But their purpose is to serve the collective, not to build a hierarchy.

The three Scrum roles and what they actually mean

  • Scrum Master — protects the team from outside interruptions, removes blockers that individuals can't clear alone, and runs the ceremonies (standups, retros, grooming). Not a manager. Not a ticket-assigner. A shield for the team's focus.
  • Product Owner — owns the backlog priority and defines what “done” means for each story. Decides the what; the team decides the how. One voice for the stakeholders so the team isn't pulled in ten directions.
  • Development Team — self-organizes around the sprint goal. No “senior dev who assigns work to juniors” — the team pulls from the backlog together and decides collectively how to tackle each story.

In a two-person indie studio these roles might all be you on different days. That is fine. The framework still works. What GYRA removes is the bureaucratic overhead those roles accumulate in enterprise environments — the approval gates, the ticket-routing ceremonies, the permission layers — none of which scale down.

What GYRA corrects is the tool assumption that one story = one person. That assumption is the thing that breaks the rugby scrum. GYRA brings it back.

The Sprint Board

The board is the most visible part of Scrum: columns representing the lifecycle of a story within a sprint.

To Do → In Progress → Done (with optional Review / QA columns depending on your workflow).

A card starts in To Do and moves right as work progresses. That's the whole mechanic. The power is in the visibility: the board makes the state of the entire sprint legible at a glance. You can see what's stuck, what's moving, and whether you'll actually finish by the sprint end.

Reading the board honestly

  • If everything is in “In Progress” for days — something is blocked or scope is too large
  • If nothing has reached “Done” by mid-sprint — stories need to be split smaller
  • If the same person always carries 8+ cards — redistribute; the sprint will fail
  • If the board doesn't match reality — update the board. It only works when it's honest.

The Retrospective

At the end of every sprint, run a quick Retrospective. Ask three questions: What went well? What didn't? What will we change next sprint? Even five minutes of honest retro accelerates team learning faster than any process document ever could.

GYRA has a Retro view in the sidebar. Use it every sprint, even if you work alone. Especially if you work alone.

The sprint board is a shared lie-detector. Cards that never move reveal process problems before they become team problems. Trust the board.
